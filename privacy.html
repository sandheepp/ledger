Ledger

Privacy Policy

Ledger reads your bank's transaction SMS on your phone and works out your spending there. The app is complete without an account. Signing in is optional, and if you use it, your bank data is stored on the Ledger server you signed in to — this page says plainly what that means.

Last updated 22 September 2026.

Ledger is a personal expense tracker. It reads the transaction SMS your bank already sends you, turns them into a spending record, and shows you where your money went. Everything it works out, it works out on your phone. Parsing, categories, splits, search, trends, the app lock and any holdings you type in yourself all run on the device, with no network connection of any kind.

There is an optional sign-in, and it is the whole privacy question. Ledger can sign in to a machine running the companion Ledger server. That machine may be the developer's hosted instance at ledger.factorylens.in, or one you run yourself. It is off until you turn it on, it is not required for any feature above, and an install that never signs in sends nothing anywhere except the daily rule download described below. If you do sign in, your bank messages and statements are stored on that machine, and whoever operates it can read them — for ledger.factorylens.in, that is the developer of this app.

What Ledger reads

Your SMS messages

This is the app's core function. A bank transaction alert — "Rs 450 spent on card XX1234 at …" — is the only record of a payment that exists on the phone, so reading it is the only way to build a spending history without you typing in every purchase.

  • Messages are read on the device and matched against a table of bank message formats.
  • A message that matches becomes a transaction: date, amount, direction, account last-four, merchant, category.
  • A message that does not match is discarded, with the one exception described under "Reporting a message format".
  • Ledger never reads your messages for any other purpose and never builds a contact graph from them.

Nothing else

Ledger does not request contacts, location, camera, microphone, call logs, photos or files. It contains no advertising SDK, no analytics SDK and no crash-reporting SDK.

Where your data lives

In the app's private storage on your phone. If you never sign in to a server, that is the only place it ever is.

Uninstalling Ledger deletes all of it. Android backup is disabled for this app, so it is not copied into your Google account backup either.

What leaves your phone

Three things, and only three.

1. Rule updates — a download, never an upload

Once a day Ledger fetches a file of bank message formats over HTTPS from GitHub, so that a bank which changes its wording is understood without waiting for an app update. It is a plain file download, and it is the app's only automatic connection to the public internet. Nothing about you is sent with it. GitHub, as the host, sees the network request in the ordinary way any website sees a visitor. The same file also carries the version number of the newest published release, which is how the app can tell you an update exists without asking anyone anything.

2. Signing in to a Ledger server — only if you choose to

Two kinds of machine can be on the other end:

  • The developer's hosted instance at ledger.factorylens.in, which anyone can create an account on. It is run by the developer of this app on a rented virtual machine.
  • A machine you run yourself — a computer on your own wifi, or a server you rent and administer — at an address you type in.

You sign in with a username and password. The app exchanges those for a sync token for your account, and from then on it can upload on your behalf:

Checked on a real 12,570-message inbox: 7,972 messages were sent as bank-looking, 4,598 were kept back on the phone, and none of the kept-back ones would have parsed as a transaction.

Be clear about what this means. Whoever operates that machine can read what is stored on it — your bank messages, your statements, your transaction history. For ledger.factorylens.in that is the developer of this app. He does not sell it, share it, advertise against it or feed it to anyone else, and there is no analytics or tracking anywhere in the system; but he administers the machine it sits on, so he can read it, and you should decide with that in mind. On a machine you run yourself, the person who can read it is you.

Accounts on one machine are separate from one another — own data, own rebuilds, own sync token — and that separation is enforced on the server rather than in the page, so no account can read another's.

An install that never signs in sends nothing here at all.

3. A message format you choose to report

If Ledger sees a message that looks financial but matches no known format, it can offer to let you report it so the format can be added. This is never automatic. You tap to share, you see exactly what will be sent, and before it is shown to you every run of two or more digits is replaced with x, every UPI handle and email address is removed, and the name you are greeted by is replaced with <name> — so amounts, dates, account numbers, reference numbers and your name are destroyed, and only the shape of the sentence survives. From the phone you then choose where to send it with your own share sheet. From a signed-in web session the masked sample instead goes to the operator of that server, labelled with your username, so they know which bank format to write.

Transport

  • ledger.factorylens.in is HTTPS, with a certificate from Let's Encrypt. So is the daily rule download.
  • A server on your own network is reached over plain HTTP, because no certificate authority issues certificates for an address like 192.168.x.x and the traffic never leaves your wifi. Ledger decides this from the address itself: private, .local and loopback addresses stay HTTP; anything else is forced to HTTPS, because the body of that request is your bank SMS and it is about to cross other people's networks.

How long it is kept, and how to delete it

On your phone: until you delete it or uninstall the app.

On a server you have signed in to: until you close the account. There is no other expiry — the point of the thing is a spending history that goes back years.

Closing the account is a button, and it is real. Profile → Close this account removes your ledger and your sign-in together: the parsed transactions, the SMS and statements you uploaded, your categorisations and the username. It asks for your password rather than accepting the session you are already holding, because a borrowed screen should not be able to delete somebody's ledger. There is no soft delete and no grace period. If a rebuild happens to be reading your data at that moment it refuses and asks you to try again in a few minutes. The operator can do the same from the machine's command line if you ask them by message instead.

What that button cannot speak for is the machine's backups. If the operator takes snapshots of the disk, a copy may survive in one until it ages out. Ask them; on ledger.factorylens.in, ask the address at the bottom of this page.

If you would rather nobody hold your data at all, do not sign in. The app is complete without it.

Who we share it with

No one. Your data is not sold, rented, brokered, shared with third parties or used for advertising. There is no third party in the system: no analytics provider, no ad network, no crash reporter, no data broker. The only parties are you, the app on your phone, and — if you signed in — the operator of the machine you signed in to.

The app lock, honestly

Ledger can require a PIN, password or fingerprint before it opens. The secret is stored as a PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 hash with a random salt, so the PIN itself is not written down anywhere, and it is never sent off the device.

Be clear about what that protects: it stops a person holding your unlocked phone from opening the app. It is not disk encryption, and not a defence against someone who takes the device apart or has root access — on such a phone the app's files can be read regardless. Android's own device encryption and screen lock are what protect you there, and Ledger's lock sits on top of them rather than replacing them.

Children

Ledger is not directed at children and we do not knowingly process children's data.

Your rights

If you have not signed in to a server, you exercise every right directly and immediately, with no request to make of anyone: view your data in the app, change or delete any entry, or uninstall to erase all of it at once. There is no account to close because you never opened one.

If you have signed in, the same holds without having to ask: read everything in the app or the web view, correct anything you disagree with, and Profile → Close this account deletes the lot. For anything that button does not answer — a question about backups, or a copy you think survived — write to the address below.
